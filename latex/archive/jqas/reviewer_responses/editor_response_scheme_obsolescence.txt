RESPONSE TO REVIEWER -- "Scheme Obsolescence" terminology (Section 4.4)

I appreciate this comment, and the reviewer's central point is well taken. I have revised Section 4.4 so that the low year-to-year persistence is attributed primarily to unobserved random variation rather than to schematic change, and I have retitled the section "Year-to-Year Persistence of Coaching WAR" to remove the implication that scheme obsolescence is the headline finding. A 16-game season carries a binomial sampling floor of about 1.94 wins, and the model's test RMSE of roughly 2.5 wins sits just above it, so a single season is an inherently noisy measurement of coaching quality. I agree that strategic evolution does not undo a coach's schematic skill from one season to the next, and I no longer frame it that way.

I would offer one qualification, however. "Scheme obsolescence" was never intended as a single-season mechanism; it refers to a slower, multi-year drift. Because winning percentage is zero-sum across the league, an advantage that diffuses to other staffs erodes the innovator's relative edge even when his own execution is unchanged, so genuine scheme obsolescence would manifest as regression toward the mean rather than as a separate statistical signature. At the multi-year horizon, NFL offensive innovations do tend to be copied and countered fairly quickly, often within one or two seasons (the Wildcat, the read-option, and more recently RPO and motion-based concepts), so I do not think the idea requires an implausibly rapid pace of change.

I should be explicit that "regression to the mean" is a description of a pattern, not a mechanism: both pure sampling noise around a stationary mean and a genuinely declining conditional mean produce the same high-then-lower data, and the autocorrelation alone cannot separate them. I have added a familiar player-level analogue to make this concrete, the quarterback "sophomore slump," in which a standout rookie often regresses once defensive coordinators have a full season of film. That decline is partly classical regression to the mean, but the "book is out" mechanism, in which opponents adapt to a previously unfamiliar approach, is a recognized contributor and is precisely the player-level counterpart of scheme obsolescence. I present it as illustration rather than proof, and I state in the text that the autocorrelation cannot by itself distinguish the two readings.

The pattern that does carry evidential weight is the one feature of the data that symmetric noise alone would not produce: offensive-background coaches show less multi-year persistence than defensive-background coaches, a gap that is strongest at the two-year horizon, where it survives Benjamini-Hochberg correction (two-year-average R^2 of 0.05 versus 0.18; Appendix E). Because the multi-year horizons are built from overlapping rolling averages and are therefore not independent, I present this as a suggestive interpretation rather than a demonstrated mechanism, and I have softened the section's language so that scheme obsolescence now reads as a speculative, secondary point.
