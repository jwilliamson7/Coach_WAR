I agree that multilevel models are a natural choice in player-level WAR literature and that the non-independence of sports data must be taken seriously. The short version of our justification is that we feed the model real, measured facts about each coach’s past performance versus their peers, rather than treating the coach as a hidden value the model must infer, and that we perform feature engineering to control for the non-iid nature of the data. These decisions make our task a high-dimensional prediction across many inputs, which is where boosted trees excel.
In response to your push, I did implement two multilevel models as suggested. One approach used the top 20 non-coaching features, and the second approach used PCA applied on all non-coaching features. These approaches yielded a correlated at rho = 0.86 and rho = 0.82, respectively. Our substantive conclusions about the best and worst coaches, therefore, do not hinge on the choice of model.
Where the approaches genuinely differ is out-of-sample accuracy. On the same held-out coaches, XGBoost reaches a test R2 of 0.43, versus 0.29/0.27 for the multilevel model approaches, and 0.15 for the Ridge regression. Non-XGBoost approaches yield a significant degradation in out-of-sample generalizability. 
We do acknowledge one genuine advantage of the multilevel approach, that it shrinks coaches with very few seasons toward the league average, where we instead report wide confidence intervals (for example, the three-season coaches in Tables 1 and 2); we flag empirical-Bayes shrinkage as a useful direction for sharpening short-tenure precision in future work.
I also do not treat observations as independent. The main source of dependence, repeated seasons under the same coach, is handled by a coach-clustered train/test split that keeps all of a coach's seasons in the same partition, so the test set consists of entirely unseen coaches and within-coach correlation cannot leak (Section 3.3). Era effects are removed by z-scoring the performance features within each season, and a chronological hold-out (train on Year <= 2014, test on Year >= 2015) gives nearly identical performance to the random split (R^2 = 0.443 vs. 0.430).
